Eating in Japan with dietary needs

  • Hidden fish stock: many Japanese dishes that look vegetarian are made with dashi — a soup stock usually made from bonito (fish) flakes.[1]
  • Mirin contains alcohol: mirin is a sweet rice seasoning legally classified as an alcoholic beverage. It is used in many sauces, including the sweet eel (kabayaki) sauce.[2]
  • Gyoza contain pork: Hamamatsu gyoza filling is mainly cabbage and pork — not suitable for vegetarians or halal diets.[3][4]
  • Ask before ordering: say clearly “no meat, no fish, no dashi” and show the phrases below. Writing them down helps.[1]
  • Each restaurant below has a Dietary information box. Green = confirmed yes/available, red = no/contains, grey = not confirmed. Always double-check with the staff.

Unagi (Eel) of Lake Hamana 浜名湖のうなぎ ★ Top pick

Hamamatsu is known as the birthplace of eel farming in Japan: Kurajiro Hattori began eel farming at Lake Hamana in the Meiji era, over 120 years ago. The area had young glass eels, a warm climate, flat land for ponds, plentiful groundwater, and feed (small fish from the lake and silkworm pupae from the local silk industry).[10][11]

  • Kabayaki = eel grilled with a sweet soy-based sauce; Hamamatsu offers both Kanto style (back-cut, steamed then grilled) and Kansai style (belly-cut, grilled without steaming)
  • Shirayaki = plain grilled eel eaten with wasabi soy sauce (no sweet sauce)
  • Dietary note: eel is a fish (not vegetarian). The kabayaki sauce typically contains soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar — i.e. alcohol-derived seasonings. Shirayaki avoids the sweet sauce but is still fish.
  • Expect ¥4,000–6,000+ per bowl at long-established shops

Lake Hamana Seafood: Oysters & Shirasu 浜名湖の牡蠣・しらす

Oysters are farmed in Lake Hamana (Maisaka, Arai, Shirasu areas) and are in season in winter. Shirasu (whitebait/baby sardines) is caught off Maisaka in the Enshu-nada sea by pairs of boats; Maisaka Port is called the birthplace of shirasu fishing.[28][29][25]

  • Oyster season: November–April per Hamamatsu City fish calendar (Nov–March per at-S); plump, rich oysters
  • Shirasu season: fishing allowed 21 March – 14 January (closed mid-Jan to mid-March); ~120 actual fishing days, mostly until November
  • Kamaage (boiled) shirasu is the classic; raw shirasu only on days with a fresh catch
  • Dietary note: seafood is not vegetarian
